## What problem do they solve in an inspection?

If you inspect electrical panels, a job site, equipment, a vehicle or a roof, you know the scene: a flashlight in one hand, a tool or a form in the other, and you need a photo of a finding *right now*. Pulling out your phone means putting something down, finding the camera, and often losing the angle you had.

Good documentation matters because the photo is what the next person will see. Photo guidelines for inspections agree on the basics: the image should show clearly what you're reporting, be labeled, and be taken in good light. The Wisconsin Department of Transportation, for example, asks in its [inspection photo best-practice guidelines](https://wisconsindot.gov/dtsdManuals/strct/inspection/Inspection%20Photo%20Best%20Practices%20Guidelines%202026.pdf) that photos be date-stamped, that the location and purpose of each one be noted, and that you avoid shooting into the sun so detail isn't lost.

Camera glasses solve one specific part of that workflow: **capture**. Your hands stay on the work and the photo comes from where you're looking.

## A simple workflow for documenting an inspection

These are general documentation habits; adapt them to your company's protocol:

1. **Define required shots before you start.** A short list (overview, each critical point, nameplates and labels) saves you a return trip for a missing photo.
2. **Shoot context first.** A wide view of the area, then the detail of the finding. A close-up alone may not show where the problem is.
3. **Vary angle and distance.** Two or three shots of the same spot take seconds and prevent doubts when you write the report.
4. **Watch the light.** Avoid shooting into the sun; with the sun to your side, details read better.
5. **Leave a voice note per finding.** While the photo is fresh, say what it is and where.
6. **Import and organize the same day.** Move the media to your phone, name it with location and purpose, and file it in your job folder.
7. **Check photos against your report.** It's your last chance to catch something you didn't record.

## Privacy and consent are part of the job

Work sites usually have people: the client, on-site staff, other technicians. Record only with people's knowledge and, where a site has photo rules (industrial plants, restricted areas, third-party property), get permission first. Avoid framing people or documents unrelated to the inspection.
